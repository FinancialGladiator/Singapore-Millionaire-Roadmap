Four things — all within reach of a minimum-wage worker — make it possible:

1 · Discipline
Live below your means for the long haul: bank the raises, skip the car, buy the cheapest flat and stay. Boring, on purpose.
2 · The grants
Leverage Singapore's scaffolding: a subsidised HDB flat, a grant that erases a third of the price, CPF, and near-zero income tax.
3 · Invest in the STI
Put every spare dollar into the index consistently, reinvest the dividends, and never flinch — for 45 years.
4 · Maximise SRS
Shelter income in the SRS every year — contribute enough and income tax can fall to as little as S$0 — while it keeps compounding in the STI until retirement.

The maths behind it
Methodology · every number, traced

How the maths
actually works.

The projection is one chain repeated 540 times (monthly, age 20→65). Here's each link, its formula, and the assumptions — then the findings that fell out.

1

Wage → CPF → take-home

Each earner starts at S$2,400/mo, growing 2.5%/yr. CPF takes 37% total; the employee's 20% comes out of pay, the rest is employer-added. What's left is spendable cash.

take_home = wage − (wage × 20%) ; CPF-OA inflow = wage × 23% (age <35)
2

Take-home → tax → living costs → surplus

Subtract income tax (≈0 for most of the career), then the frugal living budget (and child costs, for the family). What remains is the monthly surplus that gets invested.

surplus = take_home − tax − living_costs (− child_costs)
3

Housing runs on CPF, not cash

At the buy age, the 20% deposit is drawn from accumulated CPF-OA; the 80% loan is amortised over 25 years at 2.6% and paid monthly from CPF-OA. Because OA inflow exceeds the instalment, cash is untouched — so buying raises the investable surplus (rent stops), and payoff doesn't change it.

instalment = loan × r ÷ (1 − (1+r)^−300), r = 2.6%/12
4

Surplus → STI at 6%, dividends reinvested

Each month's surplus is added to the brokerage and the whole balance grows at 6%/yr. That 6% is a total return — ~3% price growth plus ~4% dividend yield, reinvested. Reinvesting dividends is what turns the low price return into 6%; it's about half the entire result.

STI ← (STI + surplus) × 1.06^(1/12) each month
5

CPF compounds in parallel

OA grows at 2.5% (after paying the flat); SA/MA/RA grow at 4%. Two earners build two separate Retirement Accounts.

OA ← OA × 1.025^(1/12) ; RA ← RA × 1.04^(1/12)
6

At 65 → retirement income

The Retirement Account is annuitised into CPF LIFE (~9.4% of the sum paid out per year, for life). The STI pot is drawn at a safe 4%/yr. No rent, because the flat is owned.

income = RA × 9.4%/12 + STI × 4%/12 (+ S$0 rent)

Key findings

1 · The scaffolding, not the wage, is the story

A minimum-wage worker retiring a millionaire only works because four Singapore levers stack: a subsidised flat, a grant wiping out a third-plus of the price, CPF forced-saving that funds both the home and a lifelong annuity, and near-zero income tax. Strip any one out and the picture changes sharply.

2 · The mortgage doesn't crowd out investing

Because the HDB loan is paid from CPF-OA rather than cash, it never competes with the STI contributions. The investing surplus jumps at purchase (when cash rent stops), not at payoff — the opposite of the usual Western intuition.

3 · Dividends do half the work

At 6% total return, reinvested dividends (~4% of the ~6%) drive roughly half the final pot. On price return alone (~3%) the outcome would be a fraction of the size. And 6% is an average — a weak final decade can gut it.

4 · Two incomes ≈ triple, not double

The couple lands at ~3× the single, because two frugal incomes buy the flat earlier, unlock a bigger grant, and throw off far more surplus in the twenties — and early money compounds the longest.

5 · A child has a precise, lifelong price

One kid costs the family ~S$2,000/mo of retirement income and ~S$580k of net worth versus the childless couple — the compounded shadow of ~S$750/mo spent during the 22 years it couldn't be invested.

And the overriding caveat

  • These are ceilings, not expectations: 45 unbroken years of full-time work, near-monastic frugality, no crash at the wrong time, no lifestyle creep, no career gaps, both partners working through the child years.
  • CPF is simplified; grant amounts are estimated from income tiers; flats are decaying 99-year leases counted only as "housing secured."
  • An illustration of how the system plus compounding behaves — not financial advice or a forecast. Verify grants, rates and CPF rules against HDB/CPF before acting.
The behaviour gap · possible ≠ probable

Everyone can.
Almost nobody does.

The first tabs proved the arithmetic works: subsidised flat, grants, CPF, near-zero tax and an index fund give every Singaporean a clean runway. Yet most never get there. The gap isn't the maths — it's the behaviour. Here are the five things that quietly break the plan.

1

Lifestyle inflation eats every raise

The projection depends on the surplus flowing into the STI for 45 years. But spending rises to meet income — hedonic adaptation makes each upgrade the new baseline, so the reward wears off and you reach for the next one. "Frugal for life" is the first assumption real life dissolves.

Absorb half of each raise into lifestyle → the single's ~S$676k pot roughly halves.
Absorbing half of every future raise into lifestyle costs you—
The fix

Freeze your lifestyle — bank every raise

When pay rises, route the increase straight to investing instead of upgrading your life. Let income climb while spending stays flat, and pour the widening gap into the index. Not deprivation — just refusing to let each raise become the new baseline.

Beats trap #1 (lifestyle inflation). Holding the line vs spending half your raises ≈ S$676k vs ~S$340k.
2

The car — a quarter-million-dollar decision, on repeat

Singapore deliberately makes cars a luxury, and financing one is the single largest wealth-destroying choice most households make — not just the price, but the price instead of investing that money, decade after decade.

Cat A COECertificate of Entitlement -- a bidding-based permit that must be won before a car can even be registered in Singapore, on top of the car's own price. Category A covers smaller/less powerful cars. alone ≈ S$126k in 2026 (~ a year's median household income). 10-year ownership ≈ S$200k–300k; depreciation alone ≈ S$15k–25k/yr. One car across adult life ≈ the entire STI pot.
Owning a car (replaced every ~10 yrs, to 65) costs you—
The fix

Delay the car — ideally forever

Singapore's public transport is world-class; a car is a deliberate luxury. Use trains, buses and occasional ride-hail; if you truly need one, buy used late in the COE cycle. Every car-free year is S$15k–25k redirected into the index.

Beats trap #2 (the car). A lifetime of car ownership ≈ the entire STI pot you're trying to build.
3

The upgrade treadmill turns cash into walls

The magic depends on staying in the cheap subsidised flat and letting the freed cash compound. Most people trade up to a bigger HDB, then chase a condo. Each move resets the mortgage, drains the CPF-OA that was compounding, and adds decades of fresh interest. Asset-rich, cash-poor.

Trading up to a ~S$1.5m condo converts liquid, dividend-paying, compoundable wealth into an illiquid, lease-decaying asset — and can add S$300k+ of interest over 30 yrs.
Upgrading to a condo costs you—
The fix

Buy the cheapest flat that works — never upgrade

Take the 2-room Flexi (single) or 3-room BTO (couple), claim the grant, move in, and stay. Resist the condo aspiration and the bigger-HDB itch. Every year you don't upgrade is a year the freed cash and CPF-OA keep compounding instead of paying fresh interest.

Beats trap #3 (upgrade treadmill). Staying keeps S$300k+ of interest & lost compounding in your pocket.
4

The money never gets invested — or badly

The 6% engine only runs if the surplus goes into equities and stays. In reality most sits in bank savings below inflation, or is never saved. And even those who invest sabotage returns — panic-selling in downturns, chasing what just went up, trying to time the market.

20 yrs to 2024: average equity investor 9.24%/yr vs market 10.35% — behaviour cost ~1.1%/yr, ~22% less wealth. Investors call direction right only ~25% of the time. Cash at ~1% vs STI 6% over 45 yrs ≈ S$300k vs ~S$2m.
Never investing — leaving it all as cash costs you—
The fix

Buy the index, then do nothing

One broad, low-cost STI (or global) ETF. Dollar-cost average every month, reinvest dividends, and — the hard part — never sell in a crash and never chase what just went up. Downturns are where the plan is made or broken. Time in the market beats timing it.

Beats trap #4 (behaviour gap). Panic & timing cost the average investor ~1.1%/yr → ~22% less wealth over 20 yrs.
5

Starting late — the input you can't buy back

Compounding is front-loaded: at 6%, a dollar invested at 20 becomes ~S$14 by 65; the same at 40 becomes only ~S$3.20. The first decade does most of the lifting. But present bias makes the future self a stranger, so people spend their twenties and start "next year."

Delay 20 → 30 (same contributions, same 6%) ≈ 40–50% smaller final pot. The frugal twenties aren't the hardest years to give up — they're the most expensive.
Starting 5 years later costs you—
The fix

Automate it on day one — pay yourself first

The day your first paycheck lands, set up a standing monthly transfer into a low-cost STI ETF — before you budget anything else. Automation removes the monthly decision and defeats present bias: you can't spend money you never see.

Beats trap #5 (starting late). Start at 20 not 30, same money → ~2× the final pot.
6

Use the system fully — and protect the plan

Claim the EHG, let CPF-OA fund the flat, and — at this income — skip SRS and CPF cash top-ups (no tax relief when you're barely taxed); invest that money instead. Then guard it all: keep 3–6 months of expenses in cash and buy cheap term insurance, so one emergency never forces a sale at the bottom.

Leverages the grants + CPF · protects against the forced-liquidation that derails most plans.
7

Marrying someone unaligned on money

Warren Buffett has said the single most important decision in building a life — financial life included — is who you marry: find a partner whose values on saving and spending genuinely match your own, and check that before the wedding, not after. Divorce is one of the most destructive events this plan can suffer, and it rarely gets modelled. Decades of joint compounding get interrupted at the worst possible moment, matrimonial assets are typically split, and rebuilding alone starts the clock over on a chunk of what took half a working life to build.

Married and investing together from 25, divorcing at 45 — splitting ~S$1.35m in joint STI+CPF roughly 50/50, minus ~S$10,000 in legal costs (Singapore's 2026 published range: ~S$1,500–3,500 uncontested, ~S$10,000–35,000+ contested), then rebuilding alone on a single income for the last 20 years — lands near S$1.92m at 65, against ~S$3.86m had the marriage continued. Roughly half.
Three ways this could go — on your own numbers Modelled as two phases: married and building wealth together from your current age, then divorcing roughly 10 years later (sooner if you're within 10 years of 65, so there's still time left to rebuild) and continuing alone to 65. A simplification of real timing, but it isolates what a divorce specifically costs on top of everything else in this app.
The fix

Align on money before the wedding, not after it

Talk about saving, spending, debt, and risk honestly and early — ideally before getting married, not years into it. A shared savings rate and a shared instinct for what "enough" looks like is worth more to this plan than almost any investment decision, precisely because it's the one risk that can undo decades of the others at once.

Assumptions above are illustrative, not a legal outcome — Singapore courts divide matrimonial assets "justly and equitably" under the Women's Charter, not automatically 50/50, and outcomes vary with the length of the marriage, contributions, and children. Child support has no fixed formula either — the estimate here follows the income-ratio approach used in the leading case ANJ v ANK, but courts weigh caregiving, needs and both parents' means too. Spousal maintenance is even less certain: courts are often reluctant to award it to a self-sufficient earner, so it's modelled here only as a possible cost to the higher earner, never as income to rely on.
8

The price tag isn't the price

A S$18,000 bag doesn't cost S$18,000 — it costs whatever that money would have become. Bought at 35 and invested in the STI instead, ordinary purchases quietly compound into the kind of numbers usually reserved for houses, because thirty years at 6%/yr turns almost anything into six figures. None of this says never buy anything nice; it says know the real price before you decide it's worth it.

A Hermès Birkin at S$18,000, bought at 35 instead of invested, is ~S$103,000 of lost retirement wealth by 65 — about 11–12 classrooms built for children in the developing world (~S$9,000 each, based on published NGO project costs) or 108 school libraries stocked (~S$950 each). A daily S$7 artisan coffee on workdays, kept up for just 10 years and then left alone to compound, quietly becomes ~S$77,000 by 65 — nobody decides to spend that; it just accumulates one coffee at a time.

Start early · automate · stay frugal · own the cheapest home and transport · buy the index · never flinch

The STI-only plan gets you to the finish line. Why this shape — six principles
1

Diversify across asset classes, not just stocks

Owning 30 Singapore companies is one bet on one asset class in one country. Equities, property, gold, bills and income each respond differently to growth, recession, inflation and deflation — so in any given season, something in the portfolio is working. Uncorrelated assets don't just smooth the ride; they let you rebalance from winners into losers, which is where extra return quietly comes from.

Your income streams end up uncorrelated too: salary + SG dividends + REIT distributions + CPF interest + coupon income — five taps that rarely run dry at once.
2

Growth and value, across currencies

Growth stocks compound earnings; value and dividend stocks pay you to wait — over long stretches each takes turns leading, so hold both and stop guessing. Spreading the portfolio across SGD income, USD/EUR/JPY-earning global companies and gold means no single central bank's decisions control your future — multi-currency dividends are a natural hedge nobody charges you for.

One world index ETF already spans ~50 currencies and both styles. Simplicity is the feature.
3

Hard assets against debasement and the debt cycle

Over the short business cycle (~5–8 years) economies expand and contract; over the long-term debt cycle (decades) debt piles up until governments quietly inflate it away — which is currency debasement. Cash and long bonds are the casualties; property, gold and productive companies are the survivors. Your flat, your gold sleeve and your equities are the debasement protection; that's also why long bonds stay minimal.

Gold's job isn't return — it's to be the asset that can't be printed.
4

Kill the fees before they kill the compounding

Fees compound exactly like returns — against you. A "small" 1.5% annual management fee on a fund or ILP consumes roughly a third of your final pot over 40 years; 2%+ takes closer to 40%. The fix costs nothing: self-directed, low-cost index ETFs at 0.07–0.22%/yr, bought through a cheap brokerage, with no adviser trail fees, no wrap fees, no insurance-linked investment products.

S$1,000/mo × 40 yrs: 6% → ~S$1.86m · 6% minus 1.5% fees → ~S$1.28m. The fee "only" cost you S$580k.
5

Squeeze every Singapore tax advantage

Singapore is one of the best places on Earth to be an investor: no capital gains tax, and SG dividends are tax-free in your hands. For global stocks, use Irish-domiciled UCITS ETFs — they cut US dividend withholding from 30% to 15% and sidestep US estate-tax exposure that US-listed funds carry above US$60k. Add SRS once you're meaningfully taxed (see the calculator), and CPF top-ups only when the relief actually bites.

Same world portfolio, wrong wrapper ≈ 0.3–0.5%/yr lost to withholding — another silent fee.
6

Rebalance every six months — mechanically

Twice a year, on fixed dates, sell whatever has grown past its target weight and top up whatever has fallen below. That's it. The calendar — not your feelings — makes you trim euphoria and buy fear, harvesting the swings of the business cycle automatically. In Singapore there's no capital gains tax to punish the trades, so rebalancing here is unusually cheap.

Six-monthly is frequent enough to capture cycles, rare enough to let winners run and keep costs near zero.
The actual goal

Maximise net income over time — not the biggest number on a screen

The end state isn't a pot; it's a set of reliable, growing, lightly-taxed income streams: tax-free SG dividends and REIT distributions, global dividends compounding inside cheap UCITS wrappers, CPF LIFE for life, interest on the dry powder — with gold and insurance standing guard so a bad year never forces a fire sale. Diversification keeps the streams flowing in every season; zero fees and Singapore's tax code make sure you keep what they produce; the six-month rebalance keeps the machine tuned. Boring, repeatable, and very hard to kill.

Glossary — every term, plain English
Plain English · every term explained

The jargon,
de-jargoned.

Every technical term used across this dashboard, explained the way you'd explain it to a friend. Definitions are plain-language versions of standard sources (Investopedia, CPF, HDB, IRAS) — check the official sites for exact rules and current figures.

Investing & markets
STI Straits Times Index
A basket tracking the 30 biggest companies listed in Singapore — shorthand for "the Singapore stock market." When people say the market rose or fell, this is usually what they mean.
ETF Exchange-Traded Fund
A single fund you buy like a share that holds many companies at once. An STI ETF (e.g. ES3 or G3B) lets you own all 30 STI companies in one cheap purchase instead of buying each stock separately.
Total return
The full gain from an investment — both the rise in price and the dividends it pays. The 6% used here is a total return, so dividends are already included.
Dividend
A slice of profit a company pays out to its shareholders, usually a few times a year. Singapore stocks are known for relatively generous dividends.
Dividend reinvestment
Using the dividends you receive to buy more shares instead of spending them, so they compound too. Roughly half the STI's long-run return comes from doing this.
Dividend yield
The yearly dividend as a percentage of the share price — a S$100 share paying S$4 a year yields 4%.
Compound interest / compounding
Earning returns on your past returns, not just your original money — a snowball that grows faster the longer it rolls. It's the engine behind every projection here.
Dollar-cost averaging
Investing a fixed amount every month whatever the price. You automatically buy more when prices are low and less when high, and never have to guess the "right time."
Safe withdrawal rate the 4% rule
A rule of thumb that you can draw about 4% of an investment pot each year in retirement without running out over a normal lifespan.
Behaviour gap
The gap between what investments earn and what investors actually earn — because people panic-sell, chase winners and mistime the market. It typically costs around 1% a year.
Sequence-of-returns risk
The danger that a crash just before or early in retirement does far more damage than the same crash later, because you're drawing down an already-shrunken pot.
Net worth
Everything you own minus everything you owe. Here it means your investments plus CPF (your home isn't counted).
CPF & retirement
CPF Central Provident Fund
Singapore's compulsory national savings scheme. A slice of every pay cheque (plus an employer top-up) goes in, earmarked for retirement, housing and healthcare.
Ordinary Account OA
The CPF pocket you're allowed to use to pay for a home. It earns about 2.5% a year.
Special / Retirement / Medisave Accounts
The other CPF pockets: SA/RA for retirement (~4%) and Medisave for healthcare. At 55 your Retirement Account (RA) is formed to fund your pension.
CPF LIFE
A national scheme that pays you a fixed sum every month for the rest of your life from age 65, funded by your Retirement Account.
Full / Enhanced Retirement Sum FRS / ERS
Target amounts of CPF savings that set how large your lifelong CPF LIFE payout will be — the more you set aside (up to the ERS cap), the bigger the monthly cheque.
CPF wage ceiling
The monthly salary cap (~S$8,000) above which no more CPF is deducted. Earn beyond it and the extra isn't subject to CPF.
Annuity
A product you pay into (or hand a lump sum) that then pays you a guaranteed income for life. CPF LIFE is an annuity.
SRS Supplementary Retirement Scheme
A voluntary account giving a tax deduction now in return for locking money until retirement. It only helps if you actually pay income tax — which low earners barely do.
Housing
HDB Housing & Development Board
Singapore's public-housing agency. Around 8 in 10 Singaporeans live in an HDB flat — subsidised, but yours to own.
BTO Build-To-Order
A brand-new HDB flat you apply for before it's built, sold at a subsidised price. The wait is usually 3–4 years.
2-room Flexi / 3-room / 4-room
HDB flat sizes, from the compact 2-room Flexi (cheapest, ~35–45 m²) up to larger 4-room family flats.
99-year lease
HDB flats are leased for 99 years, not owned forever. As the years count down the flat slowly loses value — which is why it's treated here as "housing secured," not a growing asset.
EHG Enhanced CPF Housing Grant
A government grant that pays part of your flat's price, larger for lower incomes (up to S$120k for families, S$60k for singles). It goes into your CPF and cuts what you borrow.
MOP Minimum Occupation Period
The years you must actually live in your flat before you can sell it, rent out the whole unit, or buy private property — 5 years for standard flats, 10 for Prime and Plus flats. Renting a spare room is fine during MOP; renting the whole flat out isn't.
HFE letter HDB Flat Eligibility
A letter from HDB confirming your eligibility, grant amount and how much you can borrow — required before you can apply for a BTO or resale flat. Checks your income against the relevant ceiling first.
ABSD Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty
An extra tax on top of the usual stamp duty, charged when buying a second (or later) residential property, or in some cases when keeping an HDB flat while buying a condo. It's calculated on the higher of the price or market value, and can run into six figures.
Downpayment
The upfront share of a home's price you pay yourself (here 20%), with the rest borrowed. For an HDB flat it can come from your CPF-OA.
Mortgage / amortisation
A home loan repaid in equal monthly instalments over many years. Amortisation is the steady paying-down; early payments are mostly interest, later ones mostly the loan itself.
Tax & money
GST / VAT Goods & Services Tax
A 9% tax added to most things you buy. It's baked into shop prices, so it quietly raises your cost of living.
Chargeable income
The part of your income that's actually taxed, after CPF and other reliefs are subtracted. Singapore's first S$20,000 of chargeable income is tax-free.
Income tax relief
Amounts subtracted from your income before tax is worked out (CPF contributions, earned-income relief and so on), lowering the bill.
CPF relief
Specifically the slice of income tax relief that comes from your own CPF contributions — the more you (or SRS) contribute, the smaller your chargeable income, and the less tax you owe.
MSR Mortgage Servicing Ratio
Caps HDB (and EC) loan repayments at 30% of gross monthly household income. It's the tighter of the two loan limits for public housing — TDSR usually isn't the binding one.
TDSR Total Debt Servicing Ratio
Caps all your monthly debt repayments — mortgage plus car loans, credit cards and anything else — at 55% of gross income. Applies to every property loan in Singapore, HDB or private.
Nominal vs. "today's dollars" real
Nominal is the face-value number in some future year; "today's dollars" strips out inflation so you can see what it's actually worth in current buying power.
Inflation
The gradual rise in prices over time, which erodes what each dollar buys. About 2.5% a year is assumed here.
Present value / discounting
Working out what a future sum is worth today by removing inflation (or interest). It's how S$2.05m in 2071 becomes ~S$676k in today's money.
COE Certificate of Entitlement
A permit you must buy at auction just for the right to own a car for 10 years — a big reason cars in Singapore are so expensive.
ARF Additional Registration Fee
A tax on top of a car's open market value, charged in rising tiers as the car's value goes up — the steepest single cost in the road-price breakdown for anything but the cheapest cars.
PARF Preferential Additional Registration Fee
A partial rebate of the ARF you get back when the car is deregistered before 10 years old — about half the ARF, in practice. It's why a car's depreciation is spread over the price minus this rebate, not the full price.
ERP Electronic Road Pricing
Singapore's road-toll system — gantries that charge a variable fee at peak times on busy roads, on top of everything else a car already costs.
Price-to-income ratio
How many years of a typical income it takes to buy a typical home — a quick gauge of how (un)affordable housing is.
Behaviour & budgeting
Lifestyle inflation creep
The habit of spending more as you earn more, so raises vanish into a fancier life instead of savings.
Hedonic adaptation
The way any upgrade — a nicer flat, car or phone — quickly becomes your new normal, so the happiness fades and you crave the next one.
Present bias
The wiring that makes us value a reward now far more than a bigger reward later — which is why saving for a distant retirement feels so hard.
Necessities vs. wants the 50/30/20 guide
A budgeting rule of thumb: roughly 50% of take-home on needs, 30% on wants, 20% invested. Needs are unavoidable (housing, food, transport); wants are the nice-to-haves.

Plain-language explanations for orientation, not formal or legal definitions. Rules, rates and thresholds change — confirm specifics with Investopedia for general concepts and CPF, HDB and IRAS for Singapore particulars.

Having lived in ten countries, I've never come across a system that pulls together this well.

But making full use of everything Singapore offers is genuinely complex, and most young people don't have the exposure to see how the pieces fit together — so they start late. This app exists to kickstart that: to make the levers visible, so more Singaporeans can minimise tax, maximise returns through consistent, rather boring, safe investing, and reach homeownership and millionaire status well before retirement age.

The wage examples used throughout — "Fast Food Restaurant worker," "Taxi Driver," and others — are generic, illustrative occupational categories, not references to any specific real company or brand. They're chosen to represent realistic entry-level and everyday Singapore wages, so the numbers feel grounded rather than abstract.
